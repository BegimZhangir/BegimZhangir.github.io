--- title: "Challenger and what you learn from recovering hardware" date: 2026-11-21 doc: ZB-B01 tags: [Engineering, Space, Essays] description: "How post-flight inspection of recovered Space Shuttle boosters exposed the O-ring problems behind the Challenger disaster, and why that matters for reusable rockets." ---

The Space Shuttle was initially designed to be a cheap, high-cadence reusable vehicle. In the early 1970s, NASA projected launch costs of $100 per pound (about $220/kg) and 60 flights per year by the 1980s.1 The final design, chosen in 1972, featured a reusable orbiter fitted with three Space Shuttle Main Engines, mounted to an expendable External Tank, which was fitted with a pair of reusable Solid Rocket Boosters that would provide the thrust necessary for the entire launch stack to lift off.2

These Solid Rocket Boosters were not recovered and reused in the way some younger readers may be familiar with. Unlike the reusable boosters flying now, such as Falcon 9 Stage 1, Starship Super Heavy and New Glenn Stage 1, these boosters would not propulsively burn towards and land on a pad, barge or catch tower. Instead, each booster would burn out, detach from the main tank, then deploy a pilot chute, a drogue chute and finally three main chutes before falling into the Atlantic Ocean, roughly 260 km off the coast of Cape Canaveral.3 Ships and divers then undertook the complicated operation of plugging the boosters’ nozzles, pumping out the seawater inside them and then tipping them into a horizontal position to be towed back to the Cape.4 At the Cape, crews washed out salt water and disassembled the boosters into a nose assembly, aft skirt, nozzle and four propellant-casting segments. The four segments were sent off to Utah for Thiokol to refurbish and refill them with solid rocket propellant. The other major assemblies were refurbished at KSC’s Assembly and Refurbishment Facility, as well as the Parachute Refurbishment Facility.5

Reusing the boosters this way was crucial to maintaining the ever-increasing cadence of the Shuttle program. Although the program never met its initial goal of 60 launches per year, reuse allowed 9 flights to be flown in the 9-month period from April 12th, 1985 to January 12th, 1986.6 The inspections necessary for the refurbishment of these boosters would play an incredibly important role in investigating the Challenger disaster, an event so devastating for the Shuttle that it would never reach that cadence again in the 25 years it flew after the disaster.7

The Shuttle SRB was designed to be transportable by rail from its production facility in Utah to its storage, assembly and integration facilities at Cape Canaveral. Unlike liquid fueled rockets, solid rockets need to carry their propellant all the way from the production facility to the launch site, making them much heavier than an equivalently sized liquid booster. The only way you could transport boosters of this size without exceeding the weight limits of rail is by splitting them into several segments.8 And if you want to split a rocket into several pieces, you need a mechanism that lets you piece them back together at the facility you just shipped them to. This is the job of the boosters’ field joints.

These field joints are mated together at the Vehicle Assembly Building in the Kennedy Space Center, where the Shuttle SRBs are assembled back together before being integrated with the rest of the Shuttle stack. These joints use a tang and clevis design, where one segment’s tang is inserted into another segment’s clevis. Pins are used to hold the tang and clevis segments in place. The final critical parts of the design are the two O-rings responsible for sealing in the hot, high pressure gas created by the booster’s burning propellant.9 The diagrams included in this post are probably much more effective than my description for creating a good understanding of the aft joint design, so please feel free to really stare at them.

With this brief overview of the booster’s joint design, we can now discuss the flaws in the booster’s engineering that caused the Challenger disaster.

Challenger’s final catastrophic flight was STS-51-L, the Shuttle’s 25th launch, which was initially slated for July 1985, but slipped for various reasons until it launched on the 28th of January, 1986.10 It carried seven astronauts, including schoolteacher Christa McAuliffe, who flew on the Shuttle as a part of NASA’s Teacher in Space Project.11 They launched in record cold for the Shuttle program, with a temperature of 36°F at liftoff, 15 degrees lower than at any previous launch.12 This cold made the Viton (basically a special type of rubber) O-rings in the booster joints become stiff, which resulted in a significant decrease in their ability to deform and seal against the hot gases inside the booster.13 Once the solid rocket boosters lit, the hot gas was able to rush past the O-rings, creating a stream of hot, high-pressure gas that was looking for a path into the lower pressure ambient environment. The stream of hot gas eroded the O-rings, allowing more hot gas to leak through and causing additional damage to the booster’s joint. The specific joint that failed on this launch was the right booster’s aft joint, with a puff of dark smoke visible at T+0.7s.14 However, this did not cause immediate catastrophic failure on the launchpad. The Shuttle actually made it for more than a minute before exploding. The explanation most investigators accept is that aluminum oxide and other combustion products from the burning propellant solidified against the cold steel of the joint and temporarily resealed the opening.15 If the weather had been right and the wind shear minimal, NASA might have narrowly avoided disaster. Unfortunately, the Shuttle experienced large wind shear loads as it ascended through the atmosphere, which caused the fragile aluminum oxide seal to fail, with a flame, and then a large plume, becoming visible at T+59.3s.16 This flame was directed at the External Tank and the strut holding it to the booster. The flame burnt through the tank and the strut until the booster rotated, the tank’s structure collapsed and the entire vehicle broke apart at T+73.1s. All seven astronauts onboard perished in the disaster.17

The aft joint design that caused this disaster had several deep flaws. It was built in such a way that the joints rotated by a slight amount during launch, momentarily creating a larger gap the O-rings had to fill, making them worse at sealing. This issue was called “joint rotation”.18 The leak check port in the joint created blowholes in the zinc chromate putty which was meant to insulate the O-rings from the hot gases. Instead, hot gas was able to accelerate through the blowholes and erode material from the O-rings.19 This port also pushed the primary O-ring into the wrong position to seal against the hot gases, increasing the amount of time for it to be “activated”.20 The secondary O-ring, which was meant to serve as a backup, was positioned next to the primary O-ring, which limited its ability to act as a truly redundant seal, since issues like joint rotation still affected it in the same way.21 And of course, the O-rings were bad at sealing in cold temperatures.22

The thing that shocked people following the presidential investigation into this disaster was the fact that NASA knew about every single one of these issues long before the disaster, along with the SRB’s main contractor, Thiokol.23 One of the most remembered things in popular culture about the disaster is the fact that NASA management pushed through with the launch in spite of being aware of all these risks. Discussing all the organizational issues which caused this literally did not fit into the main text of the presidential report, with important observations getting hidden away in Appendices.24 There was literally an entire book written by sociologist Diane Vaughan (who I am heavily using as a source for this post), which tried to capture more of the organizational and cultural issues, which STILL didn’t cover everything that was later revealed in the memoir of Allan McDonald, who was the director of the Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Motor Project.25 So if the whole truth cannot fit into a presidential report and a book, I’m definitely not going to fit it into a blog post. But what I can fit is something that fascinated me while reading Allan McDonald’s memoir. It’s how the process of inspecting flown booster hardware allowed them to find issues that they never would’ve found otherwise.

After recovery, the solid rocket boosters would be taken into Hangar AF at the Cape and disassembled into segments. Thiokol sent a team of four or five engineers to examine and photograph the joints of the booster.26 This procedure allowed for O-ring issues to be found very early in the program.

The first field joint with erosion was found after STS-2. At 0.053 inches, it was the deepest erosion found in any field joint before Challenger. The engineers calculated that this was within safety margin and did some testing with cut-up O-rings to check. They attributed the erosion on STS-2 to a blowhole in the putty creating a tunnel for the hot gas to erode the O-ring. After this, they changed the putty’s composition and flew the next flight. STS-3 did not show similar issues, so it was considered resolved for a while.27

On STS-6, heat was found to have reached O-rings on both of the nozzle joints recovered from the boosters, which shared a similar design to the field joints. Engineers argued this was less severe than what was seen on STS-2, changed the putty composition and kept flying.28

On STS-8, inspection showed that the lining on the inside of a booster nozzle had almost burnt through. Engineers determined it was caused by the angle at which the lining was wound, and it was considered a serious issue that was discussed at every flight review from then on. Every nozzle was measured for erosion afterwards.29

On STS-41-B, 0.040 inches of erosion in an O-ring on the left booster and 0.039 inches on the right booster were discovered in post-flight inspection. Engineers determined this was caused by blowholes in the putty again, but this time it was caused by increased pressure from the leak check port. They did not decrease the pressure, as they still considered leak checks to be more important for determining O-ring health, and they argued that the erosion was within safety margins, as well as saying erosion was “self-limiting”.30

With STS-41-C, 0.034 inches of erosion was measured on the right nozzle joint, heat near a blowhole was measured in a left aft field joint and soot was found past an igniter seal. Erosion was expected at this point and they waived it through.31

With STS-41-D, they didn’t just find erosion, they found soot between the O-ring seals, which meant hot gas was able to fully bypass the primary O-ring. A week-long task force was assembled to review the issue and concluded it was caused by the leak check port pushing the O-ring seal into the wrong side of the groove housing it, meaning it slid before sealing. They concluded soot got past it during this time.32

After a test showing a burnt-through igniter gasket, Thiokol checked previously flown booster igniters and found that four of the eight had soot behind the gasket. The supplier provided out-of-spec gaskets, so they fixed the production line and kept using good inventory for the time being.33

On STS-51-C, there was 0.038 inches of erosion on two field joints and jet black soot between the O-rings. Engineers concluded that the cold weather before launch had hardened the O-rings, making them slower to seal. The Shuttle got cleared for flight for the usual reasons, but engineers did start work on figuring out the resiliency of the O-rings as well as working on an erosion model.34

On STS-51-D, erosion was found on the primary O-rings of both nozzle joints, now reaching up to 0.068 inches. This fell within the recalculated safety margins.35

On STS-51-B, there was a full sealing failure on the left nozzle’s primary O-ring, with it eroding 0.171 inches. This was actually discovered late, nearly two months after the flight, because Thiokol’s refurbishment plant was filled with test-motor segments. Engineers did not feel as though they fully understood why this happened, with best guesses being contamination of the joint or the pressure of the leak check being insufficient for detecting an issue with the O-ring. Leak check pressure was increased, a launch constraint was placed, an O-ring task force was formed and work started on a capture feature, which would fix the joint rotation in the booster’s field joint design.36

STS-51-G, 51-I and 61-B all flew with erosion on the nozzle primary O-rings. The erosion was deemed within margins and within the experience base.37STS-61-A had soot between the primary and secondary O-ring.38STS-61-C had erosion in the left aft field joint.39

Data on erosion from these flights, as well as additional ground testing, was brought up during the review the night before launch to argue that Challenger’s final flight, STS-51-L, shouldn’t launch. Some engineers rightfully pointed out that on certain flights with cold launch conditions, O-ring erosion was increased. But ultimately, the data was unclear and presented in an insufficiently convincing way. There weren’t enough samples to support any kind of statistical analysis. The launch was approved, and the disaster happened.40

The reason all of these post-flight inspections fascinated me is that they were really the only way the engineers could’ve found out about the erosion issues. They didn’t have any pressure or particulate sensors that could be fit within the gap between the primary and secondary O-rings. It would be infeasible to instrument every square inch of the rocket. And trying to replicate real launch loads on a test stand was inconclusive. Engineers tried, but the results disagreed: Marshall’s horizontal structural tests measured joint gaps up to twice as large as Thiokol’s vertical pressure tests, and neither reproduced the sudden pressure spike of a real ignition, while a motor lying horizontally also sags under its own weight.41 Post-flight inspection really was the only way to catch these issues and improve launch reliability. Yes, the engineers at the time were not able to draw the correct conclusions immediately. But it did lead them to start working on a capture feature that likely would’ve prevented the issues seen on the Challenger disaster.42 If they had worked in an organization that allowed them to manufacture the feature as quickly as possible and then immediately start flying boosters with the feature, it could’ve actually worked out.

I’m mentioning all of this because it seems quite relevant to current rocket development. Obviously you can hear a SpaceX employee like Hans Koenigsmann say “So we’ve been learning with every single landing.”43 and figure that they must be getting some kind of data, but it’s kind of hard to imagine what kind of things those inspections actually do in terms of creating real, useful knowledge that can be used to prevent disasters. But examples like this made it apparent to me just how genuinely critical these inspections are for achieving truly high reliability. Because nobody, and I really mean nobody, would have had an inkling that the Shuttle booster seals had problems unless the hardware was recovered. Imagine how many expendable rockets have critical issues that just haven’t been discovered because it would’ve taken an extremely rare event, like the record cold at the Cape that January morning, to actually tip them over the edge.

It’s why I consider reusable rockets to be significantly superior in reliability to expendable rockets. It is simply impossible to fully determine everything that could go wrong in flight through telemetry and testing. Sometimes you have to inspect real hardware. And this compounds when you can fly an enormous number of non-critical flights, like Falcon’s and Starship’s Starlink launches, where you can afford to A/B test whether certain hardware changes actually have any causal influence on something like the effectiveness of a seal. And even more so when you can implement these changes quickly, instead of waiting for months like the Thiokol team did with their capture feature.44

Getting reliable, cheap and high-cadence spaceflight requires reusability. The designers of the Space Shuttle figured that out all the way back in the 70s. Even their deepest failures only really confirm that fact. What we’ve seen with the Vulcan rocket’s recent failures only really confirms that fact. And if you can’t learn from the past or the present, you probably aren’t going to be part of the future.
